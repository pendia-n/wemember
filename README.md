# We Remember It Differently

> You both remember the same night. Apparently, you were at different events.

## Concrete example

Two friends reconstruct the holiday when they missed a train. One blames a coffee stop; the other says they went to the wrong station.

## User flow

1. Choose **Our disastrous trip** and invite a friend or partner.
2. Each privately answers the same short questions: Who planned the journey? Why did you miss the train? What did the other person say? What happened next?
3. Answers stay hidden until both finish.
4. Reveal answers side by side: “She insisted we had time for coffee” versus “He went to the wrong station.”
5. AI identifies the contrast and asks a playful follow-up: “Was the coffee purchased before or after discovering the wrong station?”
6. Each records a final response or rebuttal.
7. Receive a split-screen mini-story: **One trip. Two incompatible accounts.**

Proposed price: **$4.99 for two people**. Show what the finished deliverable looks like before purchase; checkout timing remains unspecified.

## Participation and AI roles

People provide the memories, contradictions, and reactions. AI frames the contrasts, generates contextual follow-ups, and packages the story. It does not establish which memory is correct.

## Distinctive point

The reveal exposes an unexpected difference between two real accounts. The product is the discovery and reaction, not merely answering relationship questions or generating a love story.

## Smallest proposed version

Private paired answers, a coordinated reveal, one follow-up per person, and a shareable side-by-side story. Full generated video is unnecessary to test the core reveal.

## Competitive evidence

[Paired](https://www.paired.com) offers couples questions, games, quizzes, and daily conversations. This establishes adjacent competition, not proof that it offers the exact memory-reconstruction experience described here.

## Risks — interpretation

- **Existing competition: Medium–high.** Couples-question apps already serve adjacent needs and audiences.
- **Sherlocking: High.** An established couples app could introduce a “compare your memories” game pack.
- **Free substitute:** Friends can exchange questions in a chat. The hidden answers, reveal, and finished keepsake must improve the experience enough to justify payment.
- **Completion:** Both people must participate before the main payoff.
- **Relationship safety:** Never claim to detect lies or determine truth. Avoid betrayal, trauma, or serious disputes as entertainment prompts.
- **Defensibility:** A meaningful personal reveal does not yet constitute a durable moat.

## Recommendation and validation

Worth a small paid test and the simpler of the two preferred experiments. Test whether one person pays, gets the other person to participate, and both finish. Personally meaningful reveals are promising, but willingness to pay and differentiation remain unvalidated.

## Status and commercial assumptions

This is a proposed experience, not a validated market opportunity. Prices are test proposals, not proven willingness to pay. Risk ratings are qualitative judgments, not measured probabilities. The intended model is a playful, participatory, one-off purchase rather than a subscription or a passive upload-and-wait AI generator. One buyer covers invited participants; invitations should not require another payment. Lead with the experience, but clearly disclose AI involvement, price, deliverable, recording handling, and failure/refund terms before checkout. Do not promise policies before implementing them.

Sherlocking means an established platform or adjacent product incorporates the central feature, reducing the reason to buy this standalone product. A distinctive experience is not automatically a durable moat.
